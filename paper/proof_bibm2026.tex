\begin{abstract}
Monotonicity---the guarantee that predicted glycemic response never falls as
carbohydrate rises---is the kind of structural property that makes a model
auditable by a clinician. Every method we are aware of establishes it at
training time and verifies it \emph{offline, on a fixed model}, which is sound
only while the model is fixed. We describe \emph{Proof}, a $1\times1$-tile
fixed-point inference ASIC for the IHP SG13G2 open PDK that holds no weights at
all: every one arrives from an untrusted host, so the model can be per-patient.
That breaks the offline assumption. Fine-tuning an unconstrained network on
each held-out participant of the CGMacros cohort violates the sign condition
monotonicity depends on in 44 of 44 cases, stably across a twentyfold range of
fine-tuning budgets, so the chip checks its own precondition at the pin
interface for 20 flip-flops, carrying one sign bit and one non-zero bit per
hidden unit in registers that rotate in lockstep with the hidden activations.
We also report a defect that motivates the approach: the datapath was provably
monotone internally while the fixed-width output field truncated, so the
\emph{reported} response fell from 31{,}293 to $-31{,}209$ for a one-count rise
in carbohydrate while the true value rose. The design closes at 83.53\,\%
utilisation with zero DRC, LVS and antenna violations. All accuracy figures
come from 45 participants with no held-out validation set and should be read as
optimistically biased upper bounds.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

People managing type~2 diabetes are routinely asked to make a comparative
judgement about food: \emph{will this meal raise my blood sugar more than that
one?} Continuous glucose monitoring has made the answer measurable after the
fact, and a small model over meal macronutrients can estimate it beforehand.
What makes such a model usable rather than merely accurate is that it behave
sensibly: adding carbohydrate to a meal must never lower the predicted
response. A model that violates this is not just wrong on one input, it is
incoherent as advice, and no aggregate accuracy figure repairs that.

Monotonic-by-construction networks are a mature field, and certifying
monotonicity of a trained network is well studied. Both share an assumption
worth naming: the guarantee is established during training and checked
\emph{offline}, once, on a fixed set of weights. A device that ships one model
inherits that certificate.

Our device does not ship one model. \emph{Proof} holds no weights at all: the
65 learned parameters arrive over the pins for every inference, as 74 INT8
weight and bias bytes alongside 9 requantisation shift bytes. That began as an
area decision, since the parameters do not fit beside the datapath in a
$1\times1$ Tiny Tapeout~\cite{tinytapeout} tile on the IHP SG13G2 open
PDK~\cite{ihp}, but its consequence is that the weights can be
\emph{per patient}: the same silicon, a different model for each person. An
offline certificate covers the weight set it was computed for and nothing else.

This paper makes four claims. Per-patient weights are worth having: refitting
one parameter from about eight logged meals---three days---improves
per-participant error by a margin excluding zero, while refitting from a single
meal is measurably \emph{worse} than not personalising at all
(Fig.~\ref{fig:personalisation}). The precondition really does fail: an
unconstrained per-person refit violates the sign condition in 44 of 44
participants. It is nevertheless checkable in hardware, from the weight stream
itself, for 20 flip-flops, because both operands already cross the pins
(Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). And such a property can hold internally while breaking
at the interface---our datapath was provably monotone while the output field
truncated, and truncation wraps (Fig.~\ref{fig:r4}).

We are explicit throughout about what the evidence does not support: we do not
claim the network beats its baseline, and we do not claim monotonicity is free.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Monotonicity, established and certified.} Architectures that guarantee
monotonicity by reparameterising weights through a non-negative
map~\cite{runje2023,scalable2024} are an established family; the softplus
reparameterisation we train with is squarely in it and is not a contribution
here. Liu et al.~\cite{liu2020} instead certify a trained network by
mixed-integer programming --- offline, on a fixed model, and costly.

\textbf{Quantization versus structural guarantees.} Recent work asks whether
quantization preserves monotone-operator properties~\cite{quantmon2026}. It is
the nearest hit to ours and still distinct: monotone-operator theory rather
than input--output monotonicity, an offline norm-based certificate, and no
hardware. Formal verification of quantized networks~\cite{qvip2022} is likewise
offline, and verifies the network rather than the interface it arrives through.

\textbf{Concurrent error detection.} Accelerators are commonly protected by
algorithmic checksums descended from ABFT~\cite{huang1984}, applied to DNN
layers at low cost~\cite{ozen2020}, and by schemes targeting permanent faults
in systolic arrays~\cite{zhang2018}. The distinction that matters here is not
one of degree. \emph{Concurrent error detection asks whether the hardware
computed correctly. We ask whether this weight set admits the guarantee at
all.} In the failing case the arithmetic is perfectly correct and every output
is bit-exact against the integer reference model; nothing a checksum can see is
wrong. Run-time safety monitoring~\cite{xiang2022} is closer in spirit but
operates at system level, in software, on \emph{outputs}, not on a precondition
over incoming weights.

To our knowledge no prior hardware checks a semantic precondition on a streamed
weight set at its own interface. That rests on four targeted literature
searches, which is weak evidence; we state it as a limitation of the search
rather than as priority, and regard the concurrent-error and
run-time-monitoring literatures as the nearest neighbours.

\section{Device}

\subsection{One datapath, streamed weights}

Proof computes a two-layer quantised network,
% Was one line and 27.6 pt too wide for the column. Split rather than scaled,
% so the type size still matches the body.
\begin{align}
h &= \mathrm{ReLU}\!\left(\mathrm{clamp}_{0}^{127}\!\left((W_1 x + b_1) \gg s_1\right)\right), \nonumber \\
y &= (W_2 h + b_2) \gg s_2 ,
\end{align}
over INT8 weights and activations with power-of-two requantisation, so
rescaling is an arithmetic shift and the chip needs no second multiplier. Every
neuron of both layers is the same saturating dot product over a serial
$8\times8$ multiplier and a 24-bit accumulator; only the source of the
activations differs. In layer~1 the host supplies them, re-streaming the input
vector per hidden neuron---48 bytes rather than 6, saving 48 flip-flops at a
bandwidth cost that is free at meal timescales. In layer~2 the chip supplies
them from the hidden-activation register, following them with the constants 127
and 1 so that a bias is $v_8 \cdot 127 + v_9$ with no bias-specific logic.

\subsection{The monotonicity argument, and where it broke}

Write the pipeline for one output as a composition of the four stages above.
Each is monotone in its input: multiplication by a constant of fixed sign,
\emph{saturating} summation, arithmetic right shift, and clamping. Composing
them, $y$ is non-decreasing in the carbohydrate input $x_c$ provided that for
every hidden unit $j$
\begin{equation}
W_1[j][c] \cdot W_2[j] \;\geq\; 0 ,
\label{eq:sign}
\end{equation}
which we call the \emph{sign condition}: each unit either helps carbohydrate
raise the response or opposes it twice.

Two details of that argument are load-bearing. First, a saturating sum is
monotone and a wrapping one is not, so saturation is a safety requirement and
not merely a numerical nicety. Second, rounding mode is a red herring---every
standard mode is monotone.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig2_r4_divergence.pdf}
\caption{The property held internally and broke at the pins. Carbohydrate swept
for a weight set that \emph{satisfies} (\ref{eq:sign}): the internal value
never falls, but the truncating field wraps. Saturating it restores
monotonicity end to end.}
\label{fig:r4}
\end{figure}

The argument covers the \emph{internal} value. The host never reads the
internal value; it reads a fixed-width field. Our output fields originally
truncated. Truncation wraps, and wrapping is emphatically not monotone. A
property-directed sweep found the reported response falling from 31{,}293 to
$-31{,}209$ for a one-count rise in carbohydrate while the true internal value
rose to 34{,}327, in 55 of 400 randomised weight sets that all satisfied
(\ref{eq:sign}) (Fig.~\ref{fig:r4}). No stimulus-driven test was going to
surface this: every result was bit-exact against the integer reference model,
because the reference truncated too. Both were wrong in the same way, so
comparing them proved only that they agreed. Both fields now saturate, which is
monotone, and the study reports 0 of 400.

\subsection{Checking the precondition on chip}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig3_architecture.pdf}
\caption{Where the guard sits. Both operands of the sign condition
(\ref{eq:sign}) already cross the pins, but at different times: $W_1[j][c]$ is
the first weight byte of hidden neuron $j$, while $W_2[j]$ arrives much later,
in layer~2. Rather than store a weight, the guard keeps one sign bit
(\texttt{sgnreg}) and one non-zero bit (\texttt{nzreg}) per hidden unit in
registers that rotate in lockstep with the hidden activations (\texttt{hreg}),
so the bits for the unit being consumed reach the head exactly when that unit's
layer-2 weight arrives. The hop marks wires crossing without connecting.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure*}

Condition (\ref{eq:sign}) is a property of the weights, not of the RTL, so a
device handed a fresh weight set for every patient cannot inherit anyone's
offline proof. Proof therefore evaluates it itself, in hardware, as the weights
arrive.

The mechanism is cheap because both operands already cross the pins; the only
difficulty is that they arrive far apart in time. Carbohydrate is input~0 by
convention, so $W_1[j][c]$ is the \emph{first} weight byte of hidden neuron
$j$, while its partner $W_2[j]$ is the $j$-th weight byte of layer~2, many
bytes later. Storing the weights to compare them would cost more than the
datapath. Instead the guard stores one bit of each---the sign of $W_1[j][c]$
and whether it is non-zero---in two 8-bit registers that rotate in
\emph{lockstep} with the hidden-activation register. Because layer~2 consumes
activations from the head of that register, the matching sign and non-zero bits
are at the head of theirs at the same moment, and the comparison is an XOR, an
eight-input OR reduction and three ANDs (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).

Two subtleties are worth stating, since both are directly tested. A unit may
oppose carbohydrate \emph{twice}---negative on both sides is a non-negative
product---so a naive ``all weights positive'' check would reject valid models.
And a \emph{zero} carbohydrate weight can never violate the condition, which is
why a non-zero bit is carried beside each sign.

The sticky flag is merged with the accumulator's saturation flag onto one
output pin, all eight having been allocated already: \texttt{UNTRUSTED} means
numeric overflow \emph{or} a void guarantee. Both mean the same thing to a
caller, and the host holds the weights so it can always tell which. That merge
cost observability, and the mutation suite caught the loss within minutes---the
saturation tests happened to violate the sign condition too, so a
saturation-specific mutant could hide behind the guard.

The guard covers carbohydrate only. We built and measured a second guard for
the fibre direction and declined it: 187 flip-flops against 168, extrapolating
to roughly 87\,\% utilisation, too close to the edge for a tile that currently
routes clean, and it buys the weaker of the two claims---fibre's direction is
domain knowledge our data supports only marginally ($r=-0.051$). That guarantee
therefore still relies on the host using a constrained objective, and we say so
rather than implying otherwise.

\section{Data and Training}

We use CGMacros~\cite{cgmacros}, 45 participants with concurrent continuous
glucose monitoring and photographed, macronutrient-labelled meals. The target is
two-hour incremental area under the glucose curve. Splits are grouped by
participant throughout; a random row split would place one person's meals on
both sides and inflate every number, because individual response is exactly what
varies most.

Two undocumented data-quality problems needed handling: \texttt{Meal Type}
carries ten label strings for four meal types, so filtering on
\texttt{== 'Breakfast'} silently discards 61\,\% of breakfasts; and 52 records
(3.0\,\%) exceed the dataset's own published maxima, one fibre entry of 2830~g
against a documented 176 being enough to lift the training standard deviation
for fibre to 32~g against a median of 3~g. Filtering against the published
ranges improves out-of-fold $R^2$ from $+0.215$ to $+0.225$.

Separately, and documented in the dataset rather than discovered here: CGMacros
breakfasts and lunches are standardised test meals, the 383 usable breakfasts
containing only 6 distinct macronutrient combinations. Any breakfast-only
analysis is therefore a \emph{personalisation} study rather than a
meal-composition one, which is why we train across all meal types.

The model takes six meal features---carbohydrate, fibre, fat, protein, pre-meal
glucose, time of day---with eight hidden units. Participant-level biological
features are free in hardware, since the input count is not fixed in silicon,
but they do not earn their place: adding A1c, BMI, age, fasting glucose and
insulin together collapses $R^2$ from $+0.225$ to $-0.014$, each being fitted
from 45 points. A1c alone raises population $R^2$ to $+0.256$ but leaves
within-person ranking unchanged while \emph{reducing} the participants ranked
in the right direction from 41 to 37---and ranking is the use case. No hidden
width from 4 to 12 is distinguishable from 8 for the constrained model that
ships, so the model is data-limited rather than capacity-limited. Quantisation
scales were selected by search over a held-out fold, giving a mean float-to-INT8
error of 85 and a 95th percentile of 180 mg/dL$\cdot$min over 265 held-out meals
against a cohort mean response near 2900---though the maximum is 7305, on a
single meal.

Monotone models are trained by reparameterisation rather than penalty, so the
constraint holds exactly at every step: for constrained input $i$ with required
direction $s_i$ and a per-unit direction $d_j \in \{\pm 1\}$,
$W_1[j][i] = s_i d_j\,\mathrm{softplus}(A[j][i])$ and
$W_2[j] = d_j\,\mathrm{softplus}(c_j)$, whose product has sign exactly $s_i$
for every unit.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Per-patient weights earn their place}

Fig.~\ref{fig:personalisation} sweeps the number of meals a person logs before
their model is adapted, refitting only the output bias---one parameter, and one
the chip already supports, since the host streams that bias as two ordinary
weight bytes. Meals are taken in time order; sampling someone's future to
predict their past would inflate the result.

Personalisation has a cost of entry. Calibrating on a single noisy meal moves
pooled $R^2$ from $+0.194$ to $-0.077$ and \emph{significantly harms}
per-participant error ($+354$ [$+77$, $+631$] mg/dL$\cdot$min). Break-even is
near five meals. At eight---roughly three days of logging---the paired
improvement is $-150$ [$-285$, $-16$] mg/dL$\cdot$min, with 32 of 44
participants improved, and pooled $R^2$ reaches $+0.307$.

One parameter is the right number. An affine calibration $y' = ay + b$ stays
closed-form and monotonicity-preserving provided $a \geq 0$, but at $k=2$ it is
1870 mg/dL$\cdot$min \emph{worse} than bias-only. It also shows the constraint
is not academic: at $k=2$ the fitted slope had to be clamped at zero for 18 of
44 participants, so an unconstrained least-squares calibration would have
returned a negative slope and silently inverted the model for 41\,\% of them.

\subsection{The precondition fails in practice}

Streaming weights per patient means a different weight set per patient, so the
question is whether a realistic per-person fit satisfies (\ref{eq:sign}). We
train a population model unconstrained on the other participants, warm-start
it, and continue training on one held-out participant's own meals---the most
ordinary thing a host would do, and deliberately generous: the refit sees all
of that person's meals rather than the eight the curve above argues for.

It never satisfies the condition. All 44 scored participants yield a violating
weight set, with a median of 3 of 8 offending hidden units, and the result is
unchanged across fine-tuning budgets from 20 to 400 epochs. We note for
accuracy that the population model is already violating before any
personalisation---an unconstrained fit simply does not satisfy
(\ref{eq:sign}). Personalisation is what makes this undecidable offline: it is
not that per-person refits are uniquely bad, but that the weight set the device
runs is not the weight set anyone certified.

\subsection{What monotonicity costs}

Under grouped 5-fold cross-validation, paired across folds, constraining
carbohydrate costs $-0.007$ $R^2$ with a 95\,\% confidence interval of
$[-0.033, +0.020]$; adding the fibre direction costs $-0.025$
$[-0.062, +0.013]$. \textbf{No cost was detected, which is not the same as
free.} The interval admits a cost up to $0.033$~$R^2$, so the honest statement
is that this study is underpowered to resolve a difference that small.

For the same reason we do \emph{not} claim to beat the baseline. A depth-1
gradient-boosted baseline scores $0.059$~$R^2$ \emph{below} the unconstrained
network, at $[-0.118, -0.001]$---an interval that barely excludes zero and does
not survive correction for the roughly ten comparisons made across this project.

\subsection{Clinical behaviour}

Cross-fitting so that all 45 participants are held out exactly once
(1308 meals), out-of-fold $R^2$ is $+0.225$. The use case is within-person
ranking---\emph{should I swap this ingredient?}---and there the median Spearman
correlation is $+0.382$ (IQR $+0.215$ to $+0.495$), positive in 41 of 44
participants. By glycemic status (ADA A1c thresholds), $R^2$ is $+0.074$ for
15 healthy, $+0.077$ for 16 pre-diabetic and $+0.142$ for 14 type~2 diabetic
participants, with mean absolute error rising from 1557 to 2329
mg/dL$\cdot$min. Absolute error grows with severity, but so does the response
being predicted; per-group $R^2$ is not comparable across groups because
within-group variance differs.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{Selection bias is the dominant limitation.} Quantisation scales, hidden
width, adaptation method and constraint set were all chosen while looking at
the same cross-validation folds the results are reported on. Forty-five
participants was not enough to hold a clean cohort back. Every absolute figure
here is therefore optimistically biased and should be read as an upper bound;
the paired comparisons, which share folds and models, are the more trustworthy
quantities.

\textbf{The study is underpowered} for the effects it measures. Nothing
reported here survives correction for the number of comparisons made across the
project, which is why we claim no cost \emph{detected} rather than no cost, and
claim no advantage over the baseline at all.

\textbf{The truncation defect is not reachable on our data.} Zero of 265
held-out meals overflow the 16-bit field at any requantisation shift from 0 to
6 with real trained weights. The defect is real and appears in 55 of 400
synthetic weight sets, but we make no claim of patient harm on real data.

\textbf{The guard covers one of two claimed directions}, as discussed above.
The chip is a $1\times1$ tile fabricated on a shared multi-project wafer, and
we report pre-silicon signoff results, not measurements from returned parts.

\textbf{Novelty is asserted from four literature searches}, which is weak
evidence.

\textbf{This is not a medical device.} Every output is an estimate.

\section{Conclusion}

A monotonicity guarantee is a property of a weight set, and a device handed a
new weight set for every patient cannot inherit a certificate computed for
someone else's. The failure is not hypothetical---an unconstrained per-person
refit violates the sign condition in 44 of 44 cases---and the precondition is
checkable at the interface for 20 flip-flops, because both operands already
cross the pins. Such a guarantee can also hold throughout a datapath and still
be destroyed by the fixed-width field that reports it. Concurrent error
detection asks whether the hardware computed correctly; this asks whether the
model it was given admits the guarantee at all, and in the failing case the
arithmetic is perfectly correct.
